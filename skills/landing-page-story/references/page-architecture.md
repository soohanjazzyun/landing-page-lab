# Page architecture

Use for a full page or a structural audit. Choose the sequence from the visitor's uncertainty rather than a fixed storytelling template.

## Choose the entry point

| Reader situation | Lead with | Answer next | Likely useful action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knows the category and wants a practical tool | Specific job and product noun | Show it working, compatibility, first-use effort | Try, install, or open the example |
| Does not know the category | Familiar problem and concrete explanation | What this thing is, how it works, example output | See the workflow before committing |
| Evaluating a technical open-source tool | Supported use case, artifact, real output | Install/deployment model, limits, documentation | Install or read a runnable quickstart |
| Considering a higher-commitment purchase | Relevant customer outcome and scope | Evidence, process, fit, buying concerns | Book an appropriate conversation |
| Ready to act from a focused campaign | Continuity with that campaign's promise | Essential terms, proof, immediate next step | Complete the promised action |

If a product serves developers and nontechnical buyers, choose who this page serves. Do not assume the reader wants a sales call when the product is a self-service CLI. A download button must resolve to an actual download or explain the next step.

## Build a question sequence

Start with the questions that prevent action. Include only sections that materially answer them.

| Visitor question | Possible content | Evidence or visual |
|---|---|---|
| What is this, and is it for me? | Descriptive headline, audience context, bounded promise | A recognizable product example |
| What will I actually get? | Demonstration or annotated output | Genuine input and result, clearly labeled |
| Why change my current approach? | Specific friction and supported consequence | A workflow comparison, not an invented loss number |
| How does it work? | Concise process in the natural number of steps | Real interface or sequence |
| Can I believe it? | Relevant proof, maintainers, method, limitations | An inspectable source or attributable result |
| Does it fit my situation? | Scope, requirements, supported cases, exclusions | Actual compatibility and terms |
| What happens if I click? | Clear CTA and next-step explanation | Working destination and correct offer |

Put a compelling demonstration early when the category is hard to explain. Put proof early when trust is the main obstacle. Show important price or deployment constraints before the user invests in an action that depends on them. Do not bury a requirement that would change the decision.

## Hero decisions

A visitor should be able to recover the product category and relevant task from the headline, subheadline, and adjacent visual together. An outcome-only headline can work when the surrounding context supplies the missing product information. A clever line that requires scrolling to identify the product needs a more descriptive companion.

Offer two directions only if they resolve a real choice:

- **Descriptive:** What the product does, for whom or with what input.
- **Outcome-led:** The supported progress the reader wants, anchored by a specific subheadline.

Recommend one for the reader's awareness and the available evidence. Avoid option lists with cosmetic variations that leave the decision to the user.

CTA text names the action: “View the example,” “Install the CLI,” or “Request a quote” when those are accurate. Do not invent a free trial, imply immediate access to a waitlist product, or send all audiences to the same inappropriate sales action. Repeat the primary action where useful; there is no required number of repetitions.

## The blueprint

For each chosen section, specify:

```text
Section: A short functional name
Reader question: The uncertainty this section resolves
Headline: Actual proposed copy
Body: The copy or content structure
Visual/evidence: Existing asset or a precise proposed asset
Action: Text and destination, if needed
Reason for position: Why this follows the previous section
```

“Proposed asset” means work still to be made. Do not describe a proposed screenshot, testimonial, or integration as an existing one. Notes to the implementer stay out of visitor-facing copy.

## Audit priorities

Return findings with a location and a causal explanation:

- **Blocks understanding:** The hero never identifies the product, audiences are mixed, or the result cannot be connected to the mechanism.
- **Blocks the next action:** The CTA is ambiguous, its destination contradicts the offer, important requirements are hidden, or a key trust question goes unanswered.
- **Weakens the message:** Repeated abstractions, overly long text, unhelpful section order, or detail that does not support a buyer decision.

These labels indicate an editorial judgment, not a prediction of conversion lift. Start with the smallest change that removes the blockage. Keep useful product detail and working copy. If the actual problem is an unavailable feature, a poor offer, or a broken flow, identify it rather than pretending a new headline resolves it.

## Make the comparison fair

For an illustrative before/after, preserve product facts, offer, and audience. Explain whether design, copy, structure, or all three changed. Label fictional products and editor-authored drafts. A compelling visual comparison is an example, not an experiment. Reserve “test,” “winner,” and performance numbers for measurements actually made under stated conditions.
